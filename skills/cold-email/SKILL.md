---
name: cold-email
description: Write cold emails that get replies from busy people. Use when drafting outreach to investors, advisors, founders, or any busy contact.
---

Busy people skim. Use short paragraphs and cut repetition. Keep the warmth and specific details that make the opportunity worth joining. The email must stand alone. Use links for extra information and put contact details in the signature.

Make the subject concrete. "Seeking fundraising advice for FlightCaster (intro from John Smith)" beats "Re: fundraising advice". "Quick question about your talk at Config 2024" beats "Loved your talk".

Open naturally with shared context when you have it. Explain why the opportunity matters and why this recipient fits before making the ask. Introduce yourself when needed.

Make the ask explicit. Want a meeting? Name the topic, a length, and 2-3 times. Want an intro? Ask, and attach a self-contained blurb they can forward. Fundraising? Say so and ask to show the pitch. Skip Calendly when you ask for their time; the link puts the scheduling work on them.

Give before you get. Offer genuine enthusiasm for their work, a relevant insight, or a connection.

Check the email against four tests before you send. It is easy to skim without losing personality or persuasive detail. There is no fixed sentence or paragraph limit. It is clear who you are, through past roles or a body of work (Twitter, a paper, a repo, a blog); use the second until you can use the first. It has a value proposition for the receiver, like ideas for their company or a thoughtful response to something they wrote. It has one specific, low-friction ask they can do in seconds: "follow me on Twitter", "here is a live demo account", "a PDF of screenshots". "Help me with advice" and "thoughts on my startup?" go to the todo bucket and die there.

Do not write your name at the start; they see it in the From field. Do not repeat yourself. Proofread; typos kill credibility. Avoid jargon.

Preserve the sender's voice before optimizing for brevity: lowercase, warm, excited, specific, and ambitious. Use contractions and genuine enthusiasm, including expressive punctuation when it fits the relationship. Keep supplied details that build interest, such as shared history, geographic reach, scale, a distinctive venue, product fit, or notable collaborators. Choose what matters to this recipient rather than including every category.

Before returning the draft, compare it with the previously sent messages. If it sounds like a generic summary followed by an ask, rewrite it. The recipient should understand why this is exciting, why they specifically fit, and why the sender genuinely wants them involved.
